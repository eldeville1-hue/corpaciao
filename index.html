LESSON 01 / ORIENTATION

Begin with the question, not the headline.

A research article is an argument built from a question, methods, observations and interpretation. Its abstract is a useful map, but not a substitute for reading how the results were produced.

Before reading in detail, write down: Who or what was studied? What was compared? What outcome was measured? What conclusion is claimed?

Try it: Rewrite “Tutoring improves exam performance” as a research question: “Among which students, compared with what alternative, and measured how?”
LESSON 02 / DESIGN

Ask how the comparison was made.

Randomized experiments assign an intervention by chance, which can reduce systematic baseline differences between groups. Observational studies measure naturally occurring differences and may need additional assumptions and methods to support causal claims.

DesignCan often describeImportant caution
Randomized trialEffect of assigned intervention under study conditionsNonadherence, attrition and limited generalizability
Cohort studyOutcomes over time across exposure groupsConfounding and selection
Cross-sectional studyMeasurements at a point in timeTemporal order may be unclear

A study design is not a quality score. Evaluate implementation, missingness, measurement and the specific claim.

LESSON 03 / MEASUREMENT

Find out what the numbers actually represent.

Identify the population, recruitment process, sample size, outcome definition and measurement procedure. A large sample cannot repair a systematically biased measurement.

LESSON 04 / NUMBERS

Read estimates together with uncertainty.

Look for the effect estimate, its units, uncertainty interval and analysis population. A confidence interval summarizes a procedure's long-run coverage properties under its assumptions; it is not automatically the probability that the specific interval contains the true value.

Distinguish statistical significance from practical importance. An effect can be precisely estimated yet too small to matter, or potentially important but imprecisely estimated.

Check: Is the reported change absolute or relative? What is the baseline? Were outcomes selected before looking at the results?
LESSON 05 / INFERENCE

Write a conclusion the evidence can carry.

Separate three layers: observation (what the data show), interpretation (why it might have happened) and generalization (where it might apply). Each additional layer needs justification.

For an observational tutoring comparison, “students attending tutoring scored higher” may describe the sample. “Tutoring caused higher scores” requires stronger causal evidence.

The five-question test: What was asked? Who was studied? What was measured? What uncertainty remains? What conclusion is justified?
03 / REAL PAPER READING DESK · PRIMARY SOURCE

Read an actual
clinical trial.

Practice reading the design and interpreting a conclusion with the SPRINT randomized trial. This is a reading exercise, not medical advice.

The Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) compared two blood-pressure treatment targets in selected adults at elevated cardiovascular risk. Participants were randomly assigned to treatment strategies; the published report describes benefits as well as adverse events and important eligibility restrictions.

Read the paper, not our paraphrase alone.Use the original article to verify every answer. This page deliberately avoids reproducing tables or giving clinical recommendations. Results from a selected trial population do not automatically apply to everyone.

Reading guide / what a strong answer contains

State the population and interventions precisely; distinguish randomized assignment from blinding; report the primary estimate together with uncertainty and units; consider both benefits and harms; and qualify generalization using the actual eligibility criteria. Check every numerical answer against the linked article. The guide intentionally does not supply numbers you could copy without reading.
